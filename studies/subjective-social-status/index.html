Social Cognition

Measuring Subjective Social Status in Children of Diverse Societies

Dorsa Amir, Claudia R. Valeggia, Mahesh Srinivasan, Lawrence S. Sugiyama, & Yarrow Dunham · PLOS ONE (2019) · Ages 4–18

Explore the data 3D procedure Paper (PDF)

Research questions

  • Can a child-adapted 10-rung MacArthur Ladder reliably measure subjective social status across diverse cultural and economic environments?
  • Do children's abstract ladder placements align with concrete material comparisons of their family's house, food, money, and possessions?
  • How does perceived social standing change from early childhood through adolescence?

Subjective social status (SSS)—a person's sense of where they stand in a social hierarchy—predicts health, well-being, and behavior in adults even above objective income, yet little was known about how early children form stable status representations across societies with different levels of inequality and market integration.

Across 1,028 total youth assessments (ages 4–18) in New Haven (USA), Vadodara (India), Toba/Qom communities in Gran Chaco (Argentina), and two Shuar regions in Amazonian Ecuador (market-integrated Upano Valley and remote Cross-Cutucú), experimenters walked children through an illustrated 10-rung MacArthur Ladder worksheet (Figure 1) alongside a four-item Social Comparison Index (SCI) asking whether their family had a nicer/same/less nice house and more/same/less food, money, and things than most families. Two longitudinal cohorts in India additionally assessed test–retest stability after one week (N = 43) and one year (N = 665).

MacArthur Ladder placements showed moderate test–retest reliability in childhood (ρ = 0.47 at one week; ρ = 0.32 at one year) and tracked concrete SCI material comparisons across populations (β = 0.211, p = .004)—with the telling exception of remote Cross-Cutucú Shuar communities, where formal socioeconomic stratification is minimal. Across societies, younger children placed their families near the top of the ladder while perceived standing declined realism-ward across adolescence.

Citation: Amir, D., Valeggia, C. R., Srinivasan, M., Sugiyama, L. S., & Dunham, Y. (2019). Measuring subjective social status in children of diverse societies. PLOS ONE, 14(12), e0226550. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0226550
