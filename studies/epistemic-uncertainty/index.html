No developmental differences in preferences for epistemic versus physical uncertainty across three diverse cultures

Julia M. Smith, Hadi Mohamadpour, Jan Engelmann, Helen Elizabeth Davis, Justine Krieger, Bettina Gro Sørensen, Jeremy Koster, Soomaayeh Heysieattalab, & Dorsa Amir · Developmental Science (2025) · Ages 6–19

Explore the data 3D procedure Paper (PDF)

Research questions

  • Do children and adolescents prefer epistemic uncertainty (guessing a hidden outcome that has already occurred) over physical uncertainty (guessing before the outcome occurs)?
  • Does this preference diminish or reverse across development into adolescence?
  • Does prior cultural familiarity with randomization artifacts like dice shape uncertainty preferences?

Decisions made with incomplete information take two distinct forms: physical uncertainty, when an outcome has not yet happened (such as guessing a die roll before the throw), and epistemic uncertainty, when the outcome has physically occurred but remains hidden from the decision maker (such as guessing a die already rolled beneath an opaque cup). While hypothetical adult vignettes often find a preference for physical uncertainty, live games with Western children consistently show a preference for epistemic uncertainty—leaving open whether children outgrow this tendency or whether familiarity with dice games drives developmental change.

In this preregistered cross-cultural study led by Postdoctoral Scholar Julia M. Smith and PI Dorsa Amir, 210 children and adolescents aged 6–19 (M = 10.81) completed a live dice task across three diverse populations: urban communities in Iran (N = 100, ages 7–16) and the United States (N = 56, ages 6–12), where nearly all participants were familiar with dice, and semi-nomadic Tsimané hunter-horticulturalist communities in the Bolivian Amazon (N = 54, ages 7–19), where no participant had ever seen dice before (plus a supplementary Indigenous Mayangna sample in Nicaragua). After counting dots 1–6, practicing open rolls, and passing a comprehension check with an inverted opaque cup, participants viewed a counterbalanced two-panel sequence diagram (Figure 1), played one practice round of each game, and chose which version to play for a real prize: Roll-Then-Guess (epistemic) or Guess-Then-Roll (physical).

Overall, 60.48% of participants chose the epistemic Roll-Then-Guess game over the physical Guess-Then-Roll game (p < .001), including 64% in the United States (p < .001), 65% among Bolivian Tsimané youth (p < .001), and 56% in Iran. Crucially, preferences showed no age-related shift across childhood and adolescence (p = .090) and no difference by prior exposure to dice (p = .321), challenging developmental-reversal accounts and suggesting that preferring epistemic uncertainty in live chance events is a stable, cross-culturally robust tendency.

Citation: Smith, J. M., Mohamadpour, H., Engelmann, J., Davis, H. E., Krieger, J., Sørensen, B. G., Koster, J., Heysieattalab, S., & Amir, D. (2025). No developmental differences in preferences for epistemic versus physical uncertainty across three diverse cultures. Developmental Science, 28, e70078. https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.70078
