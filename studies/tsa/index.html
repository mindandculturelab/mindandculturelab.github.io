Study · Cooperation

Two- and three-year-olds coordinate decisions in dyads and triads

Alissa Rivero, Jared Vasil, and Michael Tomasello · Child Development (2026) · Ages 2–3

Explore the data 3D procedure Paper (PDF)

Research questions

  • When can young children coordinate decisions—not just actions—with same-aged peers in pairs and groups?
  • Does cooperative communication predict success, and do children rely on collaborative or individualistic strategies?
  • Can children generalize coordination to a new set of options without instruction?

Coordinating actions with a partner emerges soon after age two, but coordinating choices before acting is harder—especially in multi-party groups where adding even one partner sharply increases the difficulty of aligning decisions. In this preregistered pure coordination study, same-aged dyads and triads of 2- and 3-year-olds sat around a square table with a velcroed wooden box in the center. Through a clear glass lid, children could see reward coins inside (two per partner) that played music when dropped into a nearby music box. Each child had a red and a blue magnetic “key” on the table and stuck one to their own vertical side of the box, out of their partners’ view.

One hundred children participated across 40 groups (10 groups per cell: 2-year-old dyads and triads, mean age 2.52; 3-year-old dyads and triads, mean age 3.47; 50% female, 26% underrepresented minority), tested in the lab or at local preschools in Durham, North Carolina. Each child first completed individual training with adult experimenters—learning the music box, matching colors to criterion, and practicing unlocking the box—before joining peers for one group practice trial (where the box was held back for 10 seconds to encourage planning), three color test trials, and one surprise generalization trial in which the red and blue magnets were replaced with pig and cow magnets.

In dyads, both ages succeeded on most test trials (83.3% of 2-year-old trials; 96.7% of 3-year-old trials). In triads, 3-year-olds succeeded on 90.0% of test trials while 2-year-olds succeeded on 46.7%, with triads reaching dyad-like near-ceiling performance by 3.17 years of age. On the generalization trial, 10 of 10 two-year-old dyads and all 3-year-old dyads and triads succeeded, compared with just 1 of 10 two-year-old triads. Why the split? Two-year-olds mostly relied on individualistic strategies—such as sticking with their first successful color or trial and error—which work reasonably well with one partner but break down with two partners or novel keys. Three-year-olds succeeded primarily through collaborative strategies (checking partners’ choices or communicating about a specific key), and peer-directed key communication predicted success across ages, especially in triads—even up to 3-year-old dyads spontaneously alternating between red and blue across trials.

Citation: Rivero, A., Vasil, J., & Tomasello, M. (2026). Two- and three-year-olds coordinate decisions in dyads and triads. Child Development. https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag167
