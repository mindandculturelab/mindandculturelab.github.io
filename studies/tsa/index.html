How Two- and Three-Year-Olds Coordinate Decisions in Dyads and Triads

Alissa Rivero

Ages 2–3

Look at the data Poster

Research questions

  • When can children coordinate decisions in peer groups?
  • What approach do children take to coordinate?
  • Can children generalize their coordination?

These are the questions on the poster by Alissa Rivero, Jared Vasil, and Michael Tomasello. Children learned that a box would unlock when each partner used the same color key, red or blue, and they could not see the color the others chose. Each child was shown how the box works, the group practiced once, and then there were three test trials. The last trial changed the match from a color to a pig or a cow, with no further instruction.

One hundred children took part, ten groups in each condition: 2-year-old dyads, 2-year-old triads, 3-year-old dyads, and 3-year-old triads. The poster lists the sample as 50% female and 26% URM. Children were tested in the lab or at a daycare in Durham, North Carolina.

The poster’s main takeaway is that by age 3, children can coordinate decisions in groups. Across the three test trials, a Johnson–Neyman test put the age where dyads and triads succeed at similar rates at 3.15. Two-year-olds tended to open the box on their own, and 3-year-olds tended to do it together. Talking about the keys helped, particularly in triads. On the generalization trial the poster shows 10 of 10 two-year-old dyads, 1 of 10 two-year-old triads, and 10 of 10 groups at age 3.
