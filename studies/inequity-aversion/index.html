Computational Signatures of Inequity Aversion in Children Across Seven Societies

Dorsa Amir, David Melnikoff, Felix Warneken, Peter R. Blake, John Corbit, Tara C. Callaghan, Oumar Barry, Aleah Bowie, Lauren Kleutsch, Karen L. Kramer, Elizabeth Ross, Hina Vongsachang, Richard Wrangham, & Katherine McAuliffe

Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (2023) · Ages 4–15

Look at the data Experience the procedure Paper (PDF)

Research questions

  • What cognitive mechanisms underlie cross-cultural and developmental differences in children's rejection of unequal resource distributions?
  • Do cultural differences in fairness reflect differences in evaluative preference strength (drift rate v), response caution (threshold a), or prepotent bias (z)?
  • How do disadvantageous (1–4) and advantageous (4–1) inequity aversion diverge across seven societies?

Across the world, children reject disadvantageous inequity (receiving less than a peer) relatively early in childhood, whereas rejecting advantageous inequity (giving up a windfall when receiving more than a peer) emerges later and varies dramatically across societies. Yet choice proportions alone cannot distinguish whether children who reject unequal offers hold stronger fairness preferences, exercise greater response caution to inhibit selfishness, or start from different prepotent biases.

Applying hierarchical Drift-Diffusion Modeling (DDM; Figure 1) to 11,931 trial-level decisions and reaction times from over 800 children aged 4 to 15 across seven diverse societies—Canada, India, Maya communities in Mexico, Peru, Senegal, Uganda, and the United States—this study modeled how children operate the two-lever Inequity Game apparatus (Figure 2). Seated face-to-face with a peer, the child actor sees candies placed on two pivoting seesaw trays (1–1 equal, 1–4 disadvantageous DI, or 4–1 advantageous AI) and pulls either the green handle (tilting candies outward into the Actor's yellow bowl and Recipient's blue bowl) or the red handle (dumping all candies into the center red discard bowl so neither child receives anything).

Computational decomposition revealed that developmental and cross-cultural variation in both disadvantageous and advantageous inequity aversion is driven primarily by drift rate (v)—the speed and direction of moment-by-moment evaluative evidence accumulation—rather than decision threshold (a) or non-decision time (t). Culture shapes fairness not merely by teaching children to pause and inhibit impulses, but by tuning the immediate valuation of equitable versus inequitable outcomes.
