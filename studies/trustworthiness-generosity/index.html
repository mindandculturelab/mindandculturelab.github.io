Trustworthiness is distinct from generosity in children

Dorsa Amir, Winter S. Parsons, Richard E. Ahl, & Katherine McAuliffe · Developmental Psychology (2021) · Ages 5–8

Explore the data 3D procedure Paper (PDF)

When a peer first makes a costly trust move—forfeiting a sure smaller reward so the child can claim a larger one—5- to 8-year-olds return more resources than in a matched generosity control. Trustworthiness is not the same as baseline willingness to share.

Key findings

  • Children returned more after being entrusted with a partner’s tool than when they already held both pieces without peer sacrifice.
  • They also expected higher partner expectations in the trustworthiness condition.
  • The contrast isolates reciprocal trust from general generosity in early school age.

Research questions

  • When young children return resources in a Trust Game, are they motivated specifically by trustworthiness or simply by general generosity?
  • Does a peer's costly act of trust increase both how much children share and what they believe their partner expects?
  • Does sensitivity to trust strengthen between ages 5 and 8?

Classic economic Trust Games multiply an investor's transfer and measure how much the trustee returns, yet in developmental adaptations it has been unclear whether children's returns reflect genuine reciprocity toward a trusting partner or baseline Dictator-Game generosity. To isolate trustworthiness from generosity without requiring arithmetic multiplication, this preregistered study introduced a novel two-channel wooden Trust Apparatus covered in clear Plexiglass (Figure 1 in the paper).

One hundred eighteen U.S. children aged 5 to 8 (M = 6.94, 59 girls) sat before two parallel channels: a short channel holding a yellow square dish with 2 Starburst candies, and a long channel holding a blue square dish with 6 Starburst candies out of reach of a single short white rod. In the Trustworthiness condition, a peer partner forfeited their own short rod—giving up a guaranteed 2 candies—so the participant could snap both rods together with a red cylindrical connector sleeve and hook the 6-candy dish. In the matched Generosity control, the participant assembled the same two-piece long tool and retrieved the same 6 candies while the peer had no opportunity to invest or sacrifice.

Children shared significantly more candies in the Trustworthiness condition (M = 2.70 of 6, SD = 0.92) than in the Generosity condition (M = 2.12, SD = 1.07; b = 0.58, p = .002) and predicted that their partner expected a larger return when the partner had entrusted them with the tool piece—demonstrating that even 5-to-8-year-olds treat honoring trust as distinct from simple generosity.

Citation: Amir, D., Parsons, W. S., Ahl, R. E., & McAuliffe, K. (2021). Trustworthiness is distinct from generosity in children. Developmental Psychology, 57(8), 1318–1324. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001214
