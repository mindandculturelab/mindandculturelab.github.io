Decision Making

The Developmental Origins of Risk and Time Preferences Across Diverse Societies

Dorsa Amir, Matthew R. Jordan, Katherine McAuliffe, Claudia R. Valeggia, Lawrence S. Sugiyama, Richard G. Bribiescas, J. Josh Snodgrass, & Yarrow Dunham

Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (2020) · Ages 5–17

Look at the data Experience the procedure

Research questions

  • How do risk tolerance and future orientation (delay of gratification) develop across childhood in vastly different subsistence and market economies?
  • Are risk aversion and present orientation adaptive responses to ecological uncertainty and low resource buffering?
  • Within a single Indigenous society, does greater market integration shift children toward risk-seeking and future-oriented choices?

Waiting for a larger reward tomorrow or gambling on an uncertain payoff makes sense when future resources are predictable and buffered by markets, storage, and institutions—but can be costly when daily returns are uncertain. To test the socioecological origins of economic preferences across development, 400 children and adolescents completed parallel 5-trial Intertemporal Choice (Time) and Marble-Draw Risk tasks across two linked studies.

In Study 1, children in urban New Haven (USA), Vadodara (India), Indigenous Toba/Qom communities in Argentina, and Amazonian Shuar communities in Ecuador made five titrating time choices between 1 candy today versus 2–6 candies delivered tomorrow in a sealed bag, and five titrating risk choices between 1 guaranteed safe candy versus drawing blindfolded from a 50/50 jar containing one green winning marble (2–6 candies) and one red losing marble (0 candies) after three hands-on practice draws. Study 2 held cultural background constant by comparing Shuar children living in the more market-integrated Upano Valley against Shuar peers in remote forager-horticulturalist Cross-Cutucú communities.

Children in India, the United States, and Argentina were substantially more future-oriented and risk-seeking than forager-horticulturalist Shuar children, who strongly favored guaranteed immediate rewards. Crucially, Study 2 replicated this divergence within the Shuar: Upano Valley children growing up with higher market integration were significantly more willing to wait for tomorrow's rewards and take 50/50 risks than Cross-Cutucú peers—highlighting how local socioecology calibrates decision-making during childhood.

Citation: Amir, D., Jordan, M. R., McAuliffe, K., Valeggia, C. R., Sugiyama, L. S., Bribiescas, R. G., Snodgrass, J. J., & Dunham, Y. (2020). The developmental origins of risk and time preferences across diverse societies. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 149(4), 651–661. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000675
